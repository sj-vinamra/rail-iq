Number of tracks

Each track has its own conductor rail and belongs to one corridor (its own alignment). With the simple feeding mode every substation of a corridor feeds all tracks of that corridor through its feeders (paralleled at the TSS busbar); the Feeding arrangement tab sets feeders, section gaps and switches one by one. UP trains run from the lowest to the highest station; DOWN trains the other way. Skip-stop lists name stations a service runs through without stopping.

Feeding mode

Simple: every substation feeds every track of its corridor, all feeders paralleled at the busbar (the earlier model). Arranged: only the feeders, section gaps, load-break switches (LBS), bypass disconnectors and bus ties listed below are connected, so the result follows the single-line diagram.

Switch states are read from the drawing as the normal state. A gap LBS or link with substation names in “closes when out” closes automatically in a case where one of those substations is out. Design-check cases can also set switches, for example 4A-L1 + BT-4A-L1/4A-TR=closed.

Station arrangement from the Station 4A drawing

For each listed station and each corridor that has it, a rectifier group (TSS) named station-corridor is placed at that corridor's station chainage. Every track of the corridor gets a section gap at the station with an LBS (open, closes when that TSS is out), a west and an east feeder through DC breakers, and a W–E bypass disconnector (open). Rectifier groups of one station are joined by bus-tie disconnectors (open). Substations not arranged keep one plain feeder per track.

Substation equipment and where it is placed transformer, rectifier and DC panel of every substation (same rows as the Traction substations tab)

Locations are free text (room, building, level) for the diagram and schedule. The DC panel chainage is where the feeder cables start: a feeder cable length left blank is the distance from the panel to its tap plus the routing allowance. A rectifier-to-panel DC cable (length × Ω/km ÷ parallel cables, + and − loop) adds to the source resistance. Transformer uk, fault level and HV voltage left blank use the AC supply tab.

Station equipment from the PDS drawing rectifier HSCB, negative panel, OVPD, inverter assembly

From the single-line drawing: rectifier HSCB 72R (4000 A) to the +750 V busbar, main negative disconnector 89N (4000 A) and a −0 V negative busbar (2000 A) with one negative way per track (89NA…ND, 2000 A), an over-voltage protection device (OVPD) from the negative busbar to the main earth terminal, and an inverter assembly with its own HSCB and inverter transformer. Each way's current is the substation's feeder current onto that track; the inverter HSCB and transformer are checked against the inverter current. Blank = not checked.

11 kV supply and switchboards ring main units, incomers, Bus A / Bus B, bus coupler, transformer feeders

As on the drawing: Ring 1 (primary) and Ring 2 (alternate) from the supply authority's RMUs feed incomers A and B (normally closed / open) of an 11 kV switchboard with Bus A and Bus B and a bus coupler; each rectifier transformer is fed by its own breaker and cable. The HV current is the net AC load of the substations on the board (rectifier load minus inverter return) each second. With one ring lost the other incomer carries the whole board (the interlock closes the coupler). The transformer cable is checked for the HV fault with I = k·S/√t (k = 143 for Cu XLPE). The negative tie (NDS) between the negative panels is shown on the diagram; the rails are bonded, so it is not a separate path in the model.

    Method and assumptions
    
    
    
      
    Train run. Each service is integrated over a 5 m distance grid: a backward pass applies the service braking rate into every stop and speed restriction, a forward pass applies tractive effort limited to the set acceleration. Speed restrictions hold until the whole train length has cleared them. Resistance = Davis A + B·v + C·v² plus gradient (m·g·i) and curve resistance (Röckl: 650/(R−55) N/kN for R ≥ 300 m, 500/(R−30) below), averaged over the train length.
    
      
    Power. Motoring: wheel power ÷ drive efficiency (or the line-current curve × nominal voltage, scaled by effort used) + auxiliaries. Braking: electric brake effort up to the braking curve, × efficiency, capped at the regenerative power limit; friction brakes take the rest.
    
      
    Network. Every second the nodal equations are solved for substation busbars, feeder taps and train positions on each track (conductor + return rail loop resistance). Substations are Thévenin sources (no-load voltage, source resistance from regulation) with a diode: they cannot take current back. Optional inverters absorb surplus regeneration above their switch-on voltage.
    
      
    Corridors and feeding arrangement. Each corridor has its own alignment (chainage, stations, gradients, curves, speed limits); its tracks and substations are modelled on that chainage and corridors are electrically separate unless a feeder, link or bus tie joins them. In the simple feeding mode every substation of a corridor feeds every track of that corridor at its chainage. In the arranged mode only the listed feeders are connected: each feeder is a DC breaker from the busbar plus a cable to its tap on one track, west or east of a section gap; a gap breaks the conductor rail and is bridged only when its track load-break switch (LBS) is closed; bypass disconnectors join two feeder cables and bus-tie disconnectors join two busbars. A busbar stays live through a closed bus tie when its own rectifier is out. Switch states are the inputs, overridden by the case (outage, auto-close, or name=closed in a design-check line).
    
      
    Outgoing ways, return circuit and section gaps. Each DC panel has the number of outgoing ways set for it; every feeder uses one way (the next free one unless a way number is given) and spare ways are shown. The negative return runs from the running rails through the return cables and the negative return panel (NRP) to the rectifier negative; the cable resistance (length × Ω/km ÷ parallel cables) is in series with the substation source, and the rail bond chainage is where the return current leaves the rails in the EMC calculation. Insulated gaps in the conductor rail between adjacent substations split the supply sections; with no switch rating they stay open, so each section is fed from its own substation only.
    
      
    Short circuit near and far. For every connected feeder a bolted fault is placed on its track right at the feeder tap (near — the highest current, checked against the breaking capacity) and along the section the feeder protects, up to an open gap, the tap of the next substation or the line end; the point with the lowest current through this feeder is the far fault, checked against the feeder trip setting × margin with the time to reach the trip from the first-order rise. The far fault can be given its own (arc / contact) resistance on the Studies tab. The calculation workbook repeats both as hand calculations (one substation: I = U0 ÷ (R source + R feeder + r loop × distance + R fault), di/dt = U0 ÷ L, τ = L ÷ R) and as an IEC 61660-1 rectifier check (λ_D, I_kD, κ_D) beside the network values.
    
      
    Feeder and return cables. Where a substation has NRP return cable data, the return resistance is in series with its source, so the feeder resistance is the positive cable only (cable Ω/km, or the "positive cable only" value). Without NRP data the feeder carries both: the cable Ω/km is doubled for an equal negative cable, or the "+ and − cables" value is used. The return is never counted twice.
    
      
    Rectifier model for short circuits. Linear (default): no-load voltage behind the source resistance. IEC 61660-1: each rectifier follows I_kD = λ_D·(3√2/π)·c·U_v ÷ (√3·Z_N) per bridge, λ_D = √[(1 + (R_N/X_N)²) ÷ (1 + (R_N/X_N)²(1 + ⅔R_DBr/R_N)²)], inverted to a voltage–current curve and solved with the network by Newton iteration; c max for near faults, c min for far faults. The peak factor κ_D, time to peak t_pD and rise time constant τ1D come from the feeding rectifier's R_DBr and L_DBr at each fault, and the time to trip from the IEC rise curve (circuit L/R for remote faults beyond the range of the τ1D formula). The IEC curve can give more current than the linear source at large R_DBr, so the linear method is the cautious one for far faults; the formulas are reconstructed from the standard and should be confirmed against it for contractual use.
    
      
    Trains on the network. Constant-power loads, iterated to convergence; a step that does not converge in the set iterations is retried with the cut-back region linearised (Newton) and damping, so weakly fed sections still settle. Below the cut-back voltage, traction power reduces linearly to zero. Above the regeneration limit start, regenerated current reduces linearly to zero at the maximum voltage; the rest goes to the on-board rheostat.
    
      
    Ratings. Rectifier and transformer loads are compared as the worst rolling 1-minute, 15-minute and 1-hour RMS values against rating × duty multiplier. Transformer kVA = DC power ÷ (rectifier efficiency × power factor). Conductor and feeder thermal checks use the 1-hour RMS current.
    
      
    Short circuit. A bolted fault (set fault resistance) is placed every fault-step along each track with all substations feeding; the largest single-feeder contribution is compared with the breaker trip setting. Steady-state values: the rate of rise and peak need a transient study.
    
      
    AC data. Per bridge (pulse ÷ 6 bridges), commutation reactance Xc = transformer reactance (uk, load loss) + network reactance (fault level, X/R) referred to the valve side. AC fault currents use IEC 60909 with voltage factor c and κ = 1.02 + 0.98·e^(−3R/X).
    
      
    Short circuit, rate of rise. The same network is solved with inductances (conductor loop, feeder, rectifier 2·Lc per bridge plus any smoothing reactor) to give di/dt at t = 0; τ = Iss ÷ (di/dt), i(t) = Iss(1 − e^(−t/τ)); time to trip from the largest feeder contribution. Rail potential at the fault = If·Z(1 − e^(−γd)), Z = ½√(r/g), d = distance to the nearest TSS.
    
      
    Harmonics. For each second of TSS current: overlap 1 − cos μ = √2·Xc·Id/Uv; Ih/I1 from the overlap formula for uncontrolled bridges; characteristic orders kp ± 1, other 6k ± 1 orders at the residual %; Vh = Ih·h·Xnet. The percentile value is compared with the limits (IEC 61000-3-6 uses 95 %). A common bus adds TSS with the IEC 61000-3-6 summation exponents. DC ripple: ideal p-pulse values Vn = √2·Udi0/(n² − 1), current through the source and quarter-section inductance.
    
      
    EMC. Running rails of all tracks bonded, a ladder of rail resistance and rail–earth conductance every 100 m, train currents injected and the return current drawn at each feeder's track position each second, separately for each corridor. Touch voltage taken as rail potential (no body or footwear resistance credit). Stray current = average current leaving the rails ÷ track length. Static field from the conductor rail and return currents (Biot–Savart); induced voltage = 2πf·M′·L·I with M′ from geometry, psophometric weighting and cable sensitivity.
    
      
    Limits of this model. Train movement is not slowed by low voltage (power is cut back but the timetable holds), turnback / depot movements are not included, fault currents are first-order (no rectifier overshoot or arc dynamics), harmonics assume an ideal supply without background distortion or resonance, and EMC values are planning estimates to be confirmed by site measurement. The same engine runs in the Python edition (tps_engine.py) and gives the same numbers for the same inputs.
